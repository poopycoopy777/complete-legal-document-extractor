Cooper v. Ingo, Case No. 2025CV111 - Argument Section

COURT: District Court, Pueblo County, Colorado
CASE NO.: 2025CV111
FILING PARTY: Plaintiﬀ, opposing dismissal of the complaint
MATTER: Motion to dismiss for failure to state a claim (C.R.C.P. 12(b)(5))
STANDARD OF REVIEW: De novo; dismissal is proper only if the plaintiﬀ can prove no set of
facts entitling it to relief

ISSUE PRESENTED

Whether the district court erred in dismissing all four claims under C.R.C.P. 12(b)(5), where
the complaint pleads facts supporting each element of each claim and a complaint may be
dismissed only when the plaintiﬀ can prove no set of facts in support of the claim that would
entitle it to relief.

ARGUMENT

I. A complaint may be dismissed only when the plaintiﬀ can prove no set of facts
entitling it to relief, and the court applied a stricter standard than Colorado law allows.
C.R.C.P. 12(b)(5) permits dismissal for failure to state a claim upon which relief can be
granted. The inquiry the rule directs is a narrow one. The question is whether, taking the
allegations of the complaint as true and drawing every reasonable inference in the plaintiﬀ's
favor, the plaintiﬀ can prove no set of facts in support of the claim that would entitle it to
relief. If, under any circumstances the plaintiﬀ is entitled to relief, dismissal is improper. Warne
v. Hall, 2016 CO 50, para. 12, 373 P.3d 588, 593. The standard is the same one the Colorado
Supreme Court applied before the federal rules were revised, and it is the standard the Supreme
Court reaﬃrmed when it considered the question squarely. Id. at para. 16, 373 P.3d at 594.
Colorado has deliberately declined to import the federal plausibility standard of Bell Atlantic
Corp. v. Twombly, 550 U.S. 544 (2007), and Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556 U.S. 662 (2009), into Rule
12(b)(5). Warne, 2016 CO 50, para. 24, 373 P.3d at 596. The distinction is not semantic. A
plausibility inquiry invites the court to assess the strength of the plaintiﬀ's account. The
Colorado inquiry asks only whether that account, if true, states a claim the law recognizes. The
Supreme Court itself described the federal standard as one requiring enough facts to state a
claim to relief that is plausible on its face, Twombly, 550 U.S. at 570, and as a standard that
calls for the reviewing court to draw on its judicial experience and common sense, Iqbal, 556
U.S. at 679. Colorado rejected that invitation. A court that dismisses a complaint because the
pleaded facts do not yet prove the ultimate issue has engaged in precisely the weighing the rule

The standard is the mirror image of the summary judgment standard, and it is deliberately
generous to the pleader. The purpose of notice pleading under C.R.C.P. 8(a) is to give the
defendant fair notice of the claim and the grounds on which it rests. A complaint that does so is
suﬃcient, and the plaintiﬀ is entitled to discovery to develop the facts that will prove it. Dunlap
v. Colorado Springs Coach Co., 618 P.2d 517, 519 (Colo. 1980). The allocation of burdens that
governs the dismissal inquiry is the same allocation the Supreme Court described for summary
judgment under C.R.C.P. 56: the party moving for a terminal disposition must demonstrate that
there is nothing left to try, and the responding party is not required to prove its case in advance
of trial. Continental Air Lines, Inc. v. Keenan, 731 P.2d 708, 711 (Colo. 1987). A complaint is
measured by C.R.C.P. 8(a), and the general rules of pleading do not require the pleader to
anticipate and negate every defense the answer may raise. C.R.C.P. 8(c) assigns that function to
the answering party, who bears the burden of pleading and proving the aﬃrmative matters it
relies on. Sullivan v. Davis, 39 Colo. App. 306, 571 P.2d 1103, 1105 (1977).

II. Each claim required its own analysis, and a dismissal of four claims on a single
shared rationale cannot be sustained.
An order granting a motion under C.R.C.P. 12(b)(5) must proceed claim by claim. Each count
states a distinct legal theory with distinct elements, and each must be measured against its own
elements. The required analysis is the same in form for every claim: identify the elements,
identify the pleaded facts that go to each element, accept those facts as true, and ask whether a
reasonable factﬁnder could ﬁnd for the plaintiﬀ. If the answer is yes for a claim, that claim
survives and proceeds to discovery, whatever becomes of the others.
An order that dismisses four separate claims on one shared rationale has not performed that
analysis, and a reviewing court cannot determine from the order which element it found
wanting as to which claim. The defect is not harmless, because the appellate standard requires
the reviewing court to determine whether each claim independently satisﬁes the rule. A
dismissal under C.R.C.P. 12(b)(5) is reviewed de novo, and the appellate court applies the same
standard the trial court was required to apply. Bly v. Story, 241 P.3d 529, 532 (Colo. 2010). De
novo review means that the appellate court owes no deference to the district court's reading of
the pleading, and it must satisfy itself claim by claim that the dismissal was required. Warne,
2016 CO 50, para. 19, 373 P.3d at 595. Where the order does not disclose a claim-by-claim
element analysis, the dismissal cannot be aﬃrmed, and the appropriate disposition is reversal
so that the required analysis is conducted.
The court may not supply the missing analysis by reasoning that the claims rise and fall
together. Claims pleaded in the alternative are permitted, C.R.C.P. 8(a) permits a party to state
as many claims as it has, and C.R.C.P. 12(f) supplies a narrower remedy than dismissal where a
portion of a pleading is redundant or immaterial. A court that concludes some allegation

III. The court resolved disputed issues of fact and drew inferences against the plaintiﬀ.
Rule 12(b)(5) requires every reasonable inference to be drawn in the plaintiﬀ's favor. An order
that draws inferences against the pleader, or that credits the defendant's characterization of the
facts over the plaintiﬀ's, has converted a motion addressed to the suﬃciency of the pleading
into a premature trial of the merits. Where the plaintiﬀ's allegations and the defendant's version
of events conﬂict, the conﬂict must be resolved for the plaintiﬀ at this stage. Dunlap, 618 P.2d
at 519. A court may not dismiss a claim because the defendant oﬀers an explanation the
plaintiﬀ's allegations do not foreclose. To do so places on the plaintiﬀ the burden of negating
every possible defense in the complaint, a burden Colorado law does not impose and Rule
12(b)(5) expressly disclaims. Dunlap, 618 P.2d at 519. The rule that all doubts are resolved in
the pleader's favor is the pleading-stage counterpart of the rule that governs summary
judgment, under which all doubts are resolved in favor of the party opposing a terminal
disposition. Kaiser Foundation Health Plan of Colorado v. Sharp, 741 P.2d 714, 719 (Colo. 1987).
The same error appears in any reliance on material outside the complaint. If the court
considered a document, an aﬃdavit, an exhibit, or a representation of counsel that is neither
part of the complaint nor properly subject to judicial notice, it exceeded the Rule 12(b)(5)
inquiry. When matters outside the pleading are presented to and not excluded by the court, the
motion must be treated as one for summary judgment under C.R.C.P. 56, and the parties must
be given the notice and the reasonable opportunity to present the material that the rule makes
relevant. Alpine Bank v. Hubbell, 2017 COA 155, para. 14, 418 P.3d 604, 608. A dismissal
entered on a record the plaintiﬀ had no occasion to meet would violate that requirement. The
conversion requirement is not a technicality; it is what protects the plaintiﬀ's entitlement to
notice of the standard against which the pleading is being measured and to the opportunity to
respond with evidence. C.R.C.P. 56(e) then governs the form that evidence must take, and
C.R.C.P. 26(b)(1) deﬁnes the discovery the plaintiﬀ may take before the motion is decided.

IV. Dismissal should have been with leave to amend, and any deﬁciency in the pleading
is one amendment could cure.
Even where dismissal is warranted, the ordinary course is dismissal with leave to amend unless
amendment would be futile or the plaintiﬀ has already had a full opportunity to cure the
defect. C.R.C.P. 15(a) directs that leave to amend "shall be freely given when justice so
requires," and it embodies a policy of deciding cases on their merits rather than on pleading
defects. E-470 Public Highway Authority v. Revenig, 91 P.3d 1038, 1045 (Colo. App. 2004). A
dismissal that ends the litigation should rest on an express determination that no amendment
could save the claim. The liberality the rule commands reﬂects the same policy that governs a
motion for relief from judgment under C.R.C.P. 59(a) and C.R.C.P. 60, under which a party is
permitted to correct a defect so that the merits may be reached.

CONCLUSION

The complaint pleads facts that, taken as true and read in the plaintiﬀ's favor, state four claims
for relief under the notice pleading standard Colorado applies. The plaintiﬀ respectfully
requests that the Court reverse the dismissal of each claim, or in the alternative remand with
directions to permit amendment pursuant to C.R.C.P. 15(a).
